I

Theoretical Foundations

The physics behind magnetic order in low dimensionality.

1920 — 1944 2D Magnetism: Theoretical Foundations

1920

Lenz-Ising Model

Wilhelm Lenz proposes a model where spins can only point up or down ($\sigma_i = \pm 1$) — discrete symmetry. This restriction will prove key: it is what allows escape from the future Mermin-Wagner theorem.

$$ H = -J \sum \sigma_i \sigma_j $$
1925

1D Solution (Ising)

Ernst Ising solves the model in 1D: there is NO phase transition. Why? A single defect (domain wall) costs finite energy but gains infinite entropy — disorder always wins.

Ising erroneously generalized to all dimensions, setting the field back ~10 years.
1928

Heisenberg Model

Werner Heisenberg introduces vector spins ($\mathbf{S}_i$) that rotate freely in space — continuous symmetry. Unlike Ising, this freedom will render the model vulnerable: Mermin-Wagner will show that in 2D it destroys magnetic order.

$$ \hat{H} = -J \mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j $$
1936

Peierls Argument

Rudolf Peierls demonstrates that the 2D Ising model DOES have a phase transition. Central idea: at low temperature, creating a boundary between domains costs more energy than is gained in entropy — order survives.

Directly refutes Ising's generalization.
1941

Kramers-Wannier Duality

They discovered that the Ising model at high temperature is mathematically equivalent to the model at low temperature. The critical point lies where both descriptions coincide: the self-dual point.

Resultado: $T_c$ exacta sin resolver el modelo
1944

Onsager's Exact Solution

Onsager solves the 2D Ising model at zero field — 24 years of theoretical development. The solution revealed that the specific heat diverges logarithmically at $T_c$, an unexpected behavior that ushered in the modern study of critical exponents.

$$ T_c \approx 2.269 \, J/k_B $$

1966 — 1973 Theorems and Experimental Evidence

Theory 1966

Mermin-Wagner Theorem

Rigorously forbids long-range magnetic order (LRO) in 2D at $T > 0$ for systems with continuous symmetry (isotropic Heisenberg model).

In simple terms: if spins can point in any direction freely (continuous symmetry), thermal fluctuations completely disorder them in 2D. Unlike 3D, where neighbors "help" stabilize order, in a flat sheet there are not enough neighbors to resist disorder.

Consequence: Anisotropy (MAE) is required — meaning the spins must prefer a specific axis — to stabilize 2D magnetism. Breaking the continuous symmetry is the key.
Experiment 1966

Evidence: Ni(111) / Cu(111)

Ulrich Gradmann evaporates nickel atoms one by one onto a copper crystal and observes ferromagnetism in films of just 3 atomic layers (7 Å).

Critical distinction: these are epitaxial films — a thinned-down 3D metal grown on a substrate and dependent on it (strain, hybridization). It is not a van der Waals 2D material that can exist in isolation. That is why it is a precursor, not the discovery of 2D magnetism.

Ultrathin Films
Precursor — not an intrinsic 2D material
Resolution 1973

BKT Transition

Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless · Nobel 2016

Mermin-Wagner says there is no perfect order in 2D. But Kosterlitz and Thouless discovered a third state: neither perfect order nor total disorder. Neighboring spins tend to point in similar directions — not identical, but correlated. That correlation weakens slowly with distance (quasi-long-range).

Vortices: these are natural whirlpools in the spin field — points around which the spins rotate 360°. They are always born in pairs (clockwise + counterclockwise). As long as they remain bound, their effects cancel and local order survives. The BKT transition is the moment when, as temperature rises, the pairs break apart: the vortices become free and destroy the correlations.

Connection: Real 2D magnetic materials (CrI₃, Cr₂Ge₂Te₆) use anisotropy to circumvent MW, not BKT. But BKT demonstrated that 2D order was not impossible — it simply required mechanisms other than global alignment.

The Era of 2D Magnetism Abril 2017: Experimental Confirmation in van der Waals Crystals

CrI3

Universidad de Washington
Aislante

First intrinsic 2D ferromagnet: a mechanically exfoliated monolayer that maintains magnetic order without a substrate. Detected via the magneto-optical Kerr effect (MOKE).

  • 2D Ising model — strong anisotropy (easy axis out of plane).
  • Magnetism oscillates with layer count: monolayer FM, bilayer AFM, trilayer FM again.
Tc ≈ 45 K | Huang et al., Nature 546, 270–273 (2017)

Cr2Ge2Te6

MIT & U. Washington
Semiconductor

Second 2D ferromagnet, but with distinct physics: a semiconductor with nearly isotropic spins (Heisenberg), whereas CrI₃ is an insulator with rigid spins (Ising). That both are magnetic confirmed that 2D magnetism works across different symmetry classes. Stable from bilayer.

  • Heisenberg model — weak anisotropy, nearly isotropic spins.
  • Tunable Tc: a small external field suppresses fluctuations and raises the critical temperature.
Tc ≈ 30 K (bicapa) | Gong et al., Nature 546, 265–269 (2017)

Physical Properties to Explore via DFT

01

Stoner Criterion

Is the material magnetic? If the product of the exchange integral ($I$) and the density of states at $E_F$ exceeds 1, the system prefers to be ferromagnetic. First filter — if unmet, nothing else matters.

$$ I \cdot N(E_F) > 1 $$
02

Exchange ($J$ coupling)

How do neighboring spins couple? $J > 0$ → ferromagnetic (parallel), $J < 0$ → antiferromagnetic (antiparallel). Bridge between DFT and spin models (Ising, Heisenberg).

$$ \mathcal{H} = -\sum_{i,j} J_{ij} \mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j $$
03

Magnetocrystalline Anisotropy (MAE)

Which direction do the spins prefer? Determines the easy axis (⊥ or ∥ to the plane). This is precisely the anisotropy that breaks the Mermin-Wagner condition and enables 2D magnetism.

$$ \mathcal{H}_{SOC} = \xi \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{S} $$
04

Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya Interaction (DMI)

Are there non-trivial magnetic textures? DMI favors non-collinear configurations — spin spirals, skyrmions. Requires inversion symmetry breaking, present at interfaces and in 2D Janus structures.

$$ \mathcal{H}_{DMI} = \sum_{i,j} \mathbf{D}_{ij} \cdot (\mathbf{S}_i \times \mathbf{S}_j) $$
05

Transition Temperatures

At what temperature is order lost? DFT provides parameters at $T = 0$ K. To predict when magnetism is destroyed, a spin model is fed with $J$, MAE, and DMI and thermally simulated.

$$ P \propto e^{-\frac{\Delta E}{k_B T}} $$
Calculation of Néel ($T_N$) / Curie ($T_C$)
via Monte Carlo Simulated Annealing (MCSA)

Rational Design
"Assembling the Puzzle"

Why rational design?

In 2D materials, synthesis is slow, samples are microscopic, and characterization requires specialized equipment at cryogenic temperatures. One cannot experiment blindly — DFT screens candidates before the lab.

Each property forms a sequential pipeline: first we verify whether the material is magnetic, then how the spins couple, whether order survives in 2D, whether exotic textures exist, and finally at what temperature everything is lost.

What if we... changed the central piece?

The system with Ti has already been synthesized and shows magnetic order. The natural question is:
What magnetism emerges if we substitute Ti with other 3d transition metals? Each 3d element brings a different electronic configuration — varying d electrons, exchange coupling, and anisotropy. The DFT pipeline allows us to explore the entire family before stepping into the lab.

Key pattern: the magnetic moment peaks at the center of the 3d series (Mn, Fe, Cr > 17 μB) and decays toward the extremes — consistent with the maximization of exchange splitting in partially filled d shells.

FM Candidates: only V and Cr exhibit ferromagnetic order. Cr has a larger moment (17.08 μB), but V has a higher Tc (51.73 °C) — both are priority candidates.

Narrow FM window: Δe measures how much the dopant perturbs the electronic structure of the Nb host. Only Δe = 0 (V) and +1 (Cr) preserve FM — nearly isovalent substitutions. Greater perturbation → AFM or NM.

Skyrmion Racetrack Memory

Tiny magnetic whirlpools (skyrmions) travel along a nanometric track driven by current pulses. Each skyrmion stores a bit — like beads on an abacus, but at the atomic scale.

Conclusions

1

2D magnetism exists thanks to anisotropy — the loophole in the Mermin-Wagner theorem.

2

The DFT pipeline (Stoner → J → MAE → DMI → Tc) screens candidates before the lab.

3

The (Nb,M)₄C₃Tₓ MXenes offer a complete family of magnetic candidates still unexplored.

4

The ultimate goal: room-temperature 2D magnetism for next-generation spintronics.
